Which alerts get escalated?

1. Approach

Each alert comes with its own transaction history, about 499 transactions over up to 180 days. A model needs one row per alert, so the core of the work was turning each history into behavioural features, and finding out which behaviours actually matter.

  1. Understand the structure: table relations, the train/test split, target balance, and leakage.
  2. Form hypotheses from the EDA and AML domain knowledge: cash use, international flows, bursts, structuring, pass-through, and transaction sizes.
  3. Test each hypothesis as a feature group with the same 5-fold stratified cross-validation. A group was kept only if it improved out-of-fold ROC-AUC by more than the noise level (about 0.005).
  4. Final model: a 50/50 blend of LightGBM and CatBoost on 171 features, with a CV ROC-AUC of 0.645.

  • Leakage check: only 0.01% of transactions (840 of about 7.0M) are timestamped after the alert date, all within the same day. This is a date-versus-timestamp boundary effect, not future information. Features use only transactions up to the end of the alert day.

Card payments dominate, while cash and especially international transfers are rare. The mix is very similar for escalated and dismissed alerts.

Average size index by type: international +1.99, cash +0.53, bank transfer +0.14, card -0.43. Transaction types differ strongly in size, which later turned out to matter. Amounts must be analysed per type, not pooled.

3. Target distribution and time

17.2% of training alerts were escalated. Because the classes are imbalanced, we used stratified folds and ROC-AUC. Accuracy would be misleading: always predicting "dismissed" would already be 83% accurate.

Train and test cover the same period (January 2025 to December 2026) with the same monthly shape. The split is random, not temporal, so random stratified 5-fold cross-validation mirrors the test set. A time-based split would have validated against a situation the test set doesn't have.

The monthly escalation rate ranges from 13.9% to 21.2%. We added the alert month and weekday as features.

4. Transaction history before an alert

Every alert follows the same shape. First comes normal activity (about 2.9 transactions per day), then a quieter month (about 1.3 per day), then a burst of about 40 transactions within the final hour. The shape is almost identical for escalated and dismissed alerts. It most likely reflects the rule that generates alerts, so it explains why an alert fires, not whether it gets escalated. Features describing the burst lowered CV ROC-AUC (0.613 to 0.608), so we removed them.

5. Escalated vs dismissed: what actually differs?

We scored each behavioural hypothesis by its best single-feature ROC-AUC, where 0.5 means no signal.

Popular AML red flags (night activity, cash and international share, pass-through, the pre-alert burst) scored between 0.510 and 0.551. The clear winner is the typical size of a customer's bank transfers (0.575). Escalated alerts also have about 6% more transactions overall, with slightly smaller and less variable amounts.

Escalation is more common among customers with smaller typical outgoing bank transfers: 22.1% in the smallest-size decile versus 11.7% in the largest (overall 17.2%). 1.3% of alerts have no outgoing bank transfers and are not shown.

Key lesson: generic amount statistics that mix all transaction types together were weak (about 0.55). Splitting amounts by type and direction revealed the bank-transfer pattern and lifted the model from 0.613 to 0.645. Averaging across mixed groups was hiding the signal.

7. Conclusion

  • The signal is real but modest. No single feature exceeds a ROC-AUC of about 0.57. Combined, the final blend reaches 0.645 in cross-validation.
  • The alert trigger doesn't decide escalation. Every alert shows the same quiet month followed by a burst of about 40 transactions in the final hour, for escalated and dismissed alerts alike.
  • Who the customer is matters more than what triggered the alert. The strongest predictors describe normal behaviour over six months, above all the typical size of the customer's bank transfers.
  • Classic red flags were weak here. Night activity, cash and international share, and pass-through patterns carried little signal in this dataset.
  • Method: splitting by transaction type before averaging revealed the key pattern. Testing one feature group at a time against a fixed CV kept us from fooling ourselves.
Data: synthetic, transformed and localized for the hackathon. No real customer or bank records.
